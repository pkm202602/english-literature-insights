The Victorian Period (1832–1901)

The Victorian Period marks the historic reign of Queen Victoria. It was an era of massive industrial expansion, scientific advancement (like Darwin's theory of evolution), and imperial wealth. In literature, this period is famous for its moral seriousness, the rise of the English novel as a dominant form, and the continuous conflict between Science vs. Religion.

Key Literary Giants:

  • Alfred Lord Tennyson & Robert Browning: Towering poets who mastered the dramatic monologue and addressed contemporary doubts.
  • Charles Dickens: The ultimate Victorian novelist who exposed the harsh realities of urban poverty and the industrial working class (e.g., Oliver Twist, Great Expectations).
  • The Brontë Sisters: Emily and Charlotte Brontë, who brought intense psychological depth and gothic romance through Wuthering Heights and Jane Eyre.

Special Literary Movements

During the mid-to-late Victorian era, distinct sub-movements rebelled against the traditional moral codes and academic art standards of the time:

1. The Pre-Raphaelites

A brotherhood of poets and painters who rejected the mechanistic approach of academic art, looking back to the detailed, vibrant, and deeply symbolic art style before the Renaissance master Raphael.

2. Aestheticism and Decadence

A late Victorian movement that championed the philosophy of "Art for Art's Sake." It separated art from strict moral or social purposes, focusing purely on beauty, sensuality, and stylized expression.
