The Puritan Period (1620–1660)

The Puritan Period marks a time of intense religious, political, and social conflict in England. Driven by a desire to "purify" the Church of England from Catholic practices, the Puritans advocated for moral strictness, righteousness, and a simpler way of life. This movement fundamentally reshaped literature, turning it away from courtly romance and public theater toward deep moral, political, and spiritual analysis.

Key Characteristics & Shifts:

  • The Rise of Prose and Pamphlets: With political tensions running high, printing presses were filled with religious treatises, essays, and freedom-of-speech arguments.
  • Closure of the Theatres (1642): Public stages were officially banned by the Puritan Parliament due to moral concerns, bringing an end to the spectacular Renaissance drama era.
  • Focus on Individual Soul: Literature became deeply introspective, focusing on man’s direct relationship with God, sin, and salvation.

Monuments of Puritan Literature

John Milton (1608–1674)

The absolute titan of this period. Milton combined classical learning with deep Puritan faith. During this timeframe, he fought actively with his pen, writing Areopagitica (against censorship). The religious and political struggles of this era provided the emotional backbone for his epic masterwork, Paradise Lost.

John Bunyan (1628–1688)

A simple preacher who wrote one of the most famous Christian allegories in history, The Pilgrim's Progress. The book reflects the ultimate spiritual journey of a Christian fighting temptations to reach the Celestial City.
