The Dark / Barren Period (1400–1485/1500)

The Dark Period (also famously known as the Barren Period) refers to the timeframe in English literature following the death of Geoffrey Chaucer. It is called "barren" because England produced very few major literary geniuses or high-quality poetic works during this century, largely due to severe political unrest and civil war.

Major Historical Factors:

  • Political Instability: The era was heavily disrupted by the War of the Roses (a civil war between the House of Lancaster and the House of York).
  • The Decline of Poetry: Most poets of this era were imitators of Chaucer (known as the English and Scottish Chaucerians) but failed to match his creative brilliance.

Key Milestones and Silver Linings

Despite being a dark age for poetry, this era laid down crucial foundations for modern literature through two massive breakthroughs:

  • Introduction of the Printing Press (1476): William Caxton established the first printing press in Westminster, London. This revolutionized literature by making books accessible, cheap, and standardized.
  • Sir Thomas Malory: Wrote the famous prose work Le Morte d'Arthur, which compiled the definitive legends of King Arthur and his Knights of the Round Table.
  • Popular Ballads: This era saw a great rise in oral folk literature, including the famous stories of Robin Hood.
