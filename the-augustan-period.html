The Augustan Period (1700–1745)

The Augustan Period is heavily named after the classical Roman Emperor Augustus, as writers tried to replicate the refined, polished elegance of Roman literary masters. Famously called the Age of Pope, it marks the pinnacle of socio-political satire, biting irony, and classical order in England.

Key Characteristics of the Age:

  • Biting Satire: Literature was aggressively used as a tool to attack political hypocrisy, corruption, and societal vanity.
  • The Rise of Journalism: Popular periodicals like *The Tatler* and *The Spectator* emerged, shaping modern English essays and coffee-house political culture.
  • Early Novels: The initial seeds of the English novel format were successfully planted during these decades.

Monuments of Wit and Satire

1. Alexander Pope (1688–1744)

The dominant poet of the age who perfected the mock-heroic epic style. His timeless masterpiece The Rape of the Lock satirized a trivial aristocratic quarrel with the grand scale of classical epics. He is also famous for his critical poem An Essay on Criticism.

2. Jonathan Swift (1667–1745)

One of the greatest prose satirists in human history. Swift produced the savage political allegory Gulliver's Travels and the bitterly ironic essay A Modest Proposal, attacking contemporary social indifference.

3. Daniel Defoe

A pioneer journalist and fiction writer who published Robinson Crusoe (1719), which is widely celebrated as one of the very first true novels in the English language.
