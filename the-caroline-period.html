The Caroline Period (1625–1649)

The Caroline Period is named after King Charles I (derived from 'Carolus', the Latin name for Charles). This period was marked by growing political friction between the King and the Parliament, which eventually led to the English Civil War. In literature, the era is most famous for the distinct clash of culture between the courtly refinement and religious seriousness.

Key Characteristics & Themes:

  • Cavalier vs. Puritan: A cultural division between the Royalists (Cavalier poets) who supported the king and the religious Puritans.
  • Themes of Carpe Diem: The Cavalier poets wrote extensively about the concept of "seize the day," focusing on love, youth, beauty, and loyalty to the crown.
  • Decline of Drama: Playwriting faced major challenges, culminating in the closure of all public theatres in 1642.

The Cavalier Poets

The **Cavalier Poets** were a group of writers associated with the royal court. They wrote lighter, melodic, and polished verses. The major Cavalier poets include:

  • Robert Herrick: Famous for his lyric poetry and the iconic poem "To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time".
  • Richard Lovelace: Known for his courtly poems like "To Althea, from Prison".
  • Sir John Suckling: A prominent courtier credited with inventing the card game Cribbage and writing witty verses.
  • Thomas Carew: Known for his highly sensual and beautifully crafted love lyrics.

Prose Writers

This period also saw major developments in English prose. **Sir Thomas Browne** produced unique, highly stylistic philosophical works such as Religio Medici, blending scientific curiosity with deep religious faith.
