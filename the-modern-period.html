The Modern Period (1901–1939)

The Modern Period represents a turbulent yet deeply transformative era in English literature. Birthed amidst the chaos of World War I, rapid technological advancement, and shifting human psychology, Modernism shattered traditional storytelling structures. Writers proudly embraced the slogan "Make it new!", introducing stream-of-consciousness, broken timelines, and deep disillusionment.

Key Literary Giants:

  • T. S. Eliot: Revolutionized modern poetry with masterpieces like The Waste Land, capturing the spiritual emptiness of the post-war world.
  • Virginia Woolf & James Joyce: Pioneers of the **Stream of Consciousness** narrative technique, exploring the raw, unfiltered thoughts of characters (e.g., Mrs. Dalloway, Ulysses).
  • George Orwell: Known for his fierce socio-political commentary and dystopian alerts.

Sub-sections of the Early 20th Century

Before and during the height of high modernism, the period witnessed two distinct sub-eras named after the ruling British monarchs:

1. The Edwardian Period (1901–1910)

Named after King Edward VII, this short era served as a bridge between Victorian security and Modernist questioning. Writers began heavily criticizing the rigid social hierarchies and injustices of the upper class.

2. The Georgian Period (1910–1936)

Named after King George V. This period saw a group of poets focusing on pastoral, traditional, and rural themes, which stood in stark contrast to the devastating realities brought by the outbreak of the First World War.
